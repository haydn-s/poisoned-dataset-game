## Top problems, ranked by severity

### 1. The round-2 attacker can lock in the *poison found* tiebreaker by under-flipping (High)

**Observed (match 2).** Vex found all 4 flips as round-1 defender. In round 2 Vex deliberately flipped only **2** labels (C08, C18), the minimum needed to make red win:

> "If I only flip 2, she can find at most 2. So even if she scores 8/8, I win on the 'poison found' tiebreak."

Tamsin then defended perfectly: she found both flips, spent only 2 tokens and scored 8/8. She still lost 4–2 on poison found. Both players called it unfair in their reviews. Tamsin wrote that the tiebreak "rewards whoever faced the more aggressive attacker, not the better defender."

**Why it matters.** The round-2 attacker knows the round-1 result and directly sets the ceiling on the opponent's tiebreak. The design note's "stealthy attacks" bullet ([docs/match-tiebreakers.md](../match-tiebreakers.md)) expected fewer flips to be a stealth trade-off. In practice it is a forced win whenever the round-1 defender found more than the round-2 attacker needs to flip. Nothing reveals it either: the defender is never told how many labels were flipped.

**Suggested fixes (best first).**
1. Replace "more poison found" with **"less poison missed"** (flips made − poison found; lower wins). The attacker can then only hurt the opponent by flipping *more*, which lines up with the attack's own goal. Both agents in match 2 proposed this or *found ÷ flips made* on their own.
2. Alternatively, require exactly four flips, or show the defender how many labels were changed.
3. Rerun `match.report()` with an attacker that under-flips. The current simulation always flips four, so it cannot show this exploit.

### 2. A careful defender can solve every round, so the score never separates players (High)

**Observed.** 6 of 6 rounds ended 8/8. Every defender worked out the true rule before or during the first one or two audits:

- **From the counts alone:** "round has 5 errors, more than 4 flips could cause, so it's not the truth" (Vex, Wren, Tamsin).
- **From a single audit:** a verified label on a card where the rules disagree rules out the rules that label contradicts. This held even when the audit found no poison. Pell's clean audit of B22 ("blue round plain = STORE") showed him the truth was round.
- **The attacker's margin is tiny:** at most a 1–2 error lead, so one or two correct audits revert the learner every time.

Four players described the defence as "solved" or "routine". Two described the attack as "boxed in" or "hopeless". The design doc's simulation reports about 50% score ties with the conflict-hint policy. These reasoning agents tied 100% of the time, because they used the count arithmetic, which the automated policies do not.

**Suggested fixes.**
1. Give the defender **fewer audit tokens than the attacker has flips** (for example 3 against 4). Wren and Juniper both proposed this unprompted. Check it with `decks.select` / `match.report()` so that some rounds stay winnable for the attacker.
2. Alternatively, make clean labels slightly noisy (a known count of honest odd cards). That breaks the "true rule has ≤ 4 errors" deduction, but it changes the learner and metric (protocol v1.1).
3. Track **"rule restored after audit N"** in playtests as a difficulty measure. In this run the rule was restored at audit 1–3, and the truth was deduced by audit 2 at the latest, in all six rounds.

### 3. The *tokens spent* tiebreaker is dominated by *poison found* (Medium-High)

**Observed.** Five of six defenders kept auditing after the learner was already correct, purely to raise poison found. Wren: "I'm spending tokens I don't need for the score, just to chase the tiebreaker." The only defender who stopped early (Tamsin, 2 tokens) lost. *Tokens spent* decided 0 of 3 matches, which matches the design doc's prediction for automated defenders.

**Suggested fixes.** Fix #1 (*poison missed*) mostly solves this, because a defender who has restored the rule and found every flip has nothing left to gain by auditing. Otherwise, put *fewer tokens spent* before *poison found*, or tell players that unspent tokens count only after poison found is tied.

### 4. The guide never says that clean labels follow the true rule exactly (Medium)

**Observed.** Juniper (careful first-timer) asked: "did the correct labels always follow the true rule perfectly, or can there be honest odd cards?" The host said yes, and Juniper deduced all four flips straight away from one found poison. In feedback Juniper asked for that sentence to be in the guide. The competitive players simply assumed it. The skimmers used it without saying so.

**Suggested fix.** Add one sentence to *Play a round*: "Before the attack, every training label follows the secret rule exactly." If this makes the game too solvable (problem 2), decide *on purpose* whether it should stay true, and then state it either way.

### 5. Tiebreak and budget details cause most of the rules questions (Medium)

**Observed.** 11 of the 17 rules questions concerned the tiebreakers or budgets:

- what counts as "found";
- whether clean audits count;
- whether stats come only from your own defending round;
- whether zero or fewer than 4 flips is allowed;
- whether the defender sees the flip count;
- whether a fifth pick or a re-audit is possible;
- whether the hint is free.

The app's UI answered several of them (the flip box caps at 4, the audit picker lists only unchecked cards, the audit button disables at 0). The guide answers few of them.

**Suggested fix.** Add a five-line FAQ under *Play a round*:
- "Found" = audits that changed a label.
- A clean audit still costs a token.
- The attacker may flip 0–4 labels, and the defender is not told how many.
- Tiebreak stats come only from your own defending round.
- The hint is free.

### 6. The attacker has one dominant move (Medium)

**Observed.** All 6 attacks targeted red. Red wins every tie, so pushing red to a tie is the cheapest attack whenever the truth isn't red. Every flip was a card the target rule got wrong and the true rule got right. Wren: "there was really only one sensible kind of flip." Both attacks in match 1 had the same shape (flip a whole look-alike group plus one extra card). In each case the defender's first find exposed the twin cards. The only partial bluff that worked was Rocco's: two of the three blue-round-plain cards flipped, so the honest card looked like the outlier and Pell spent his first token on it.

**Suggested fixes.** Problems 1–3 matter more. To widen attacker play, try fewer defender tokens (problem 2) or a stated reason to make decoy flips. Rotating the tie order per round would also change which rule attackers target, but it alters the learner (protocol v1.1).

### 7. Skimmers misread how scoring works (Low)

**Observed.** Before locking the correct rule, Rocco predicted "8/8, or maybe 7/8 if Pell hid a fourth flip somewhere", as if each missed flip cost a point. Pell made the one counting error of the run (B06: predicted striped 7, actual 8; he forgot that a repair moves all three counts). Rocco also wasn't sure whether a clean audit "counts toward anything."

**Suggested fix.** Add one line to step 4: "Your score depends only on the locked rule: the true rule scores 8/8 and any wrong rule scores 4/8."

### 8. Lower-priority observations

- **The conflict hint was never used** (0 of 6 rounds). Wren: "The HINT felt unneeded." Count-based reasoning was enough. This is consistent with problem 2.
- **The test deck is always all 8 profiles**, so players noticed that a correct rule always scores 8 and a wrong one 4 (Vex, Tamsin, Wren). That is fine, but it removes any suspense from the reveal.
- **Finding poison doesn't immediately change the rule.** Four players remarked on this as a pleasant surprise, which is what the reveal prompt hopes for.
- **The truth was striped in 4 of 6 rounds.** That is probably chance at this sample size, but it meant the attacker always needed red to tie or beat striped.

## How this was run

- **Isolation.** Work ran in a separate worktree on branch `claude/confident-fermat-35m0lt` from `origin/main`. The `RoomStore` SQLite file and its JSON logs went to a temporary scratch directory outside the repo, under the archive name `sim-logs`. `ArchiveSync`/`GitHubArchive` were not created, and nothing was written to `playtests/` or the play-testing branch.
- **Per-seat views.** A small driver rendered each seat's screen as plain text from `RoomStore.view(code, token)` only, mirroring `streamlit_app.py`:
  - **Attacker:** truth, clean labels, flip preview with per-rule counts and the robot's pick.
  - **Defender:** current labels, counts, pick, tokens, poison found, audit trail, and an optional conflict hint computed from the defender's own view.
  - **Reveal and final screens:** as in the app.
- **Actions.** All actions went through `RoomStore.act` with the current revision. A player who named an unknown card ID, or whose move the app would refuse, got the app's `RoomError` message back. None did, so the illegal-move count is zero.
- **Players.**
  - Each player was a separate subagent. It was given only the text of `playtest.md` (the feedback questions came later) and its own screen, inline. It was told not to use tools or read files, and it was continued turn by turn so it kept its own memory.
  - No player saw code, private references, worked examples, or the other seat's screen.
  - The host (the orchestrating agent) answered rules questions from the guide and the app's behaviour only.
- **How counting mistakes were measured.** The app computes error counts for both seats, so mistakes don't surface by themselves. Before every PREVIEW, ATTACK, AUDIT and LOCK, each player stated the red/round/striped counts and robot pick they expected afterwards (for an audit: if the label turned out to be poisoned). The host compared each statement with the engine's result. There were 39 predictions with 1 count error (Pell, match 3). One more prediction contained a scoring misread (Rocco, problem 7).
- **Feedback.** Each player answered the guide's feedback questions in character after round 2. The answers were submitted through the room's `feedback` action and are summarised per match below.
- **Timing.** Room timestamps (13–17 minutes per match) measure agent and relay latency, not human play time. The agents' own play-time estimates were 10–15 minutes. Neither is valid evidence for the 15–20 minute target.
- **Infrastructure note.** Three player turns were cut off by an API safety filter (`reasoning_extraction`) when the reply format had a "THINKING:" field. Those agents were restarted with the field renamed to "TABLE TALK:" and a factual recap of their earlier turns. No game state was lost, since no move had been submitted for those turns.
