Who changed the labels? A tabletop game and a paired simulation of label poisoning under scarce audits

[Author name] · Explainable AI · Duke University · Fall 2026

1The question

With only four trusted checks, does auditing conflicting look-alike labels protect a learner better than checking at random?

If someone can change training labels, a model can learn a rule that fits its data and fails in use. Checking every label costs too much, so defenders choose which few to verify. Changing labels before training is a poisoning attack in NIST’s adversarial ML taxonomy.

2The game

Two players, two rounds, roles swap. Cargo cards show public features; labels are separate hang-tags that the attacker may flip.

Poison: flip up to 4 tags
Inspect: count errors
Audit: up to 4 tokens
Lock: fewest errors
Deploy: 8 new cargo

Ties between rules go red, then round, then striped. Each player defends once; the better deployment score wins.

A printed game, not only a script

Moon-colony art generated from code; a fixed-deck edition with truth and reference cards needs no per-game printing.

3The learner

The robot learns one rule: the one with the fewest errors against the current tags. A wrong rule scores 4/8 on the deployment deck, the true rule 8/8.

4Threat model

Attacker knowsClean labels, true rule, candidate rules, tie order
Attacker canFlip at most k labels, once, before any audit
Defender seesFeatures and current labels; buys trusted labels
Trusted oracleAlways right; one card per token
Test deck8 fresh records, kept out of training and audits

Two audit policies

  • Random: check any unchecked card.
  • Conflict: check a card whose identical look-alikes carry the other label, contradictions of verified cards first; recompute after each check.

Method

Protocol v1.0 frozen before the run: 200 main seeds × 2 regimes × 2 attacks × 5 flip budgets × 3 audit budgets = 36,000 runs, each policy facing the same poisoned labels. 95% percentile bootstrap over seeds.

7Limits

  • Three rules, 24 cards, a perfect oracle at uniform cost.
  • A heuristic attacker that ignores the defense; flipping whole look-alike groups could erase much of the edge.
  • Simulated policies, not measured human play.
